**By late afternoon, the harsh grey light softened into a warm, low amber.**

The heavy monsoon air cooled into a gentle breeze that rustled the cloth curtains, carrying the faint scent of rain-drenched jasmine and damp brick from the courtyard below. Misti reached back into the wooden box, her fingers brushing against the envelopes and fabric pouches before drawing out the second envelope and its matching pouch.

As she unsealed the envelope and opened the small cloth flap, a small, solid weight dropped softly into her lap.

It was a block of dark mahogany wood, no larger than her palm, with two tiny holes drilled cleanly through the center. The wood felt very smooth beneath her thumbs, smelling faintly of oil and warm timber. It bore no obvious link to the brass hook from the first letter. Misti pressed the cool wooden piece against her cheek, soaking in its density, before unfolding the paper.

> _Misti,_
>
> _If the first letter made you smile, this one is meant to help you slow down. I know how your busy mind works—you are probably turning that wooden block over in your hand right now, trying to figure it out, planning three steps ahead like you always do._
>
> _Do you remember that freezing January afternoon in North Kolkata? The power failed across the entire neighborhood, leaving us in the dark chill for hours. Instead of complaining about the cold, we dragged our heavy wool quilt right up to the window frame, held steaming cups of hot tea in our numbed hands, and read comic books out loud until the streetlights came back on._
>
> _You told me that afternoon that you wished life could always be that quiet—that you hated how fast everything moved. You spent so many years after that making sure my life was safe, taking on hard corporate shifts so I never had to worry about rent or art supplies._
>
> _Don't forget that you deserve a quiet harbor too. Put down your phone, let the afternoon be lazy, and just breathe._
>
> _Still leaning on your shoulder,_  
> _Bristi_

Misti rested her head back against the wall and closed her eyes, letting a long, slow breath slip through her lips. 

Outside, the evening was waking up—a hawker calling out fresh fruit, the distinct click of a rickshaw bell, and the soft rustle of wet palm leaves in the garden. 

She rubbed the smooth mahogany block between her palms, letting the quiet warmth of her sister's memory settle deep into her chest.
